Dutch is spoken by about 24 million people as their first language: in the Netherlands, in Flanders (the northern half of Belgium) and in Brussels, and also in Suriname and the Dutch Caribbean. It's **one language**, with one official spelling (the *Groene Boekje*) that the Netherlands, Belgium and Suriname agree on together through the *Nederlandse Taalunie*. But just like British and American English, the Netherlands and Flanders differ in pronunciation, vocabulary and a few points of grammar.

## One standard: Algemeen Nederlands

The shared norm is *Algemeen Nederlands* (AN, "General Dutch"; older people still say ABN). It's what you hear on the news, read in newspapers and learn at school in both countries. It has some room for regional words: Belgian Dutch (*Belgisch-Nederlands*) has its own standard words, and dictionaries mark them as such. People often call the Dutch of Flanders *Vlaams* (Flemish), but it's simply Dutch, with a Flemish accent and flavour.

Everyday speech in Flanders is often *tussentaal* ("in-between language"): informal Dutch with Flemish features, somewhere between the standard and the local dialects. Real dialects, like West Flemish, can be hard to follow even for other Flemish people.

## Pronunciation

| sound | Netherlands | Flanders |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *g*, *ch* | harsh, scraped at the back of the throat (in the north and west) | soft *g*, also used in the south of the Netherlands |
| *w* | between English *v* and *w* | close to English *w* |
| *v*, *z* at the start of a word | often sound like *f*, *s* (*fis* for *vis*) | stay voiced |
| *ei/ij* | often wide, almost like English "eye" (especially younger speakers) | closer to "ay" |

Flemish speech often sounds softer and more melodious to Dutch ears; Dutch speech sounds faster and more direct to Flemish ears.

## Words

Some everyday words are different. Many Flemish words are standard in Belgium (*gsm*, *hesp*), while others belong to informal speech (*goesting*, *amai*).

| Flanders | Netherlands | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| goesting | zin | fancy, desire |
| amai! | jeetje!, wauw! | wow!, oh my! |
| de frigo | de koelkast | fridge |
| de gsm | de mobiel | mobile phone |
| dikwijls | vaak | often |
| schoon | mooi | beautiful |
| plezant | leuk | fun, nice |
| ambetant | vervelend | annoying |
| de hesp | de ham | ham |
| de confituur | de jam | jam |
| het pintje | het biertje | glass of beer |
| op kot | op kamers | living in a student room |

Watch out for *schoon*: in the Netherlands it means "clean". And *dikwijls* does exist in the Netherlands, but it sounds old-fashioned there.

- Amai, wat een schoon huis! — Wow, what a beautiful house!
- Ik heb goesting in frietjes. — I fancy some fries.
- Steek de melk maar in de frigo. — Just put the milk in the fridge.
- Ik heb mijn gsm thuis laten liggen. — I've left my phone at home.
- Hij komt dikwijls te laat. — He's often late.

And typical Netherlands words you won't hear much in Flanders:

- Dat is hartstikke leuk! — That's really great!
- Ik heb zin in patat. — I fancy some chips.

## ge and gij

In informal Flemish (and in North Brabant in the Netherlands), people say *gij* (stressed) or *ge* (unstressed) instead of *jij/je*. The verb ends in *-t*, also in questions, and the object and possessive forms are *u* and *uw*. In standard written Dutch in Belgium, *jij/je* is used.

- Gij zijt zot! — You're crazy!
- Hebt ge dat gezien? — Did you see that?
- Komt ge ook naar het feest? — Are you coming to the party too?
- Wilt ge een pintje? — Fancy a beer?

Flemish people also use the polite *u* more widely than Dutch people, who switch to *jij* quickly, even with strangers.

## Diminutives: -je or -ke

The Netherlands uses *-je* and *-tje* (*het mannetje*, *het biertje*). Informal Flemish often uses *-ke* or *-ken*: *het manneke*, *het boekske*, *efkes* (*even*), as in the famous Brussels statue *Manneken Pis*. *Pintje* is the exception: it's the normal word everywhere in Flanders.

## Small grammar differences

Flanders prefers the participle first in a verb cluster (*dat ik het gezien heb*); the Netherlands often says *dat ik het heb gezien*. Flemish speech also uses *zijn* with *zitten, staan, liggen* (*ik ben daar gezeten*), the regular past *zegde* (for *zei*), and the polite *Ik had graag…* when ordering. These are explained in the articles on verb clusters, strong verbs and *zou*.

## Attitudes

Flemish people generally understand Dutch people very well, partly because they grow up watching Dutch television. The other way round can take a little more effort, and Flemish TV programmes are sometimes subtitled in the Netherlands, much to the amusement (or annoyance) of the Flemish. There are friendly stereotypes on both sides: the Dutch are seen as direct and loud, the Flemish as polite and indirect.

For learners, both are equally "real Dutch". Learn the standard language of the place where you live or that you'll visit, and get used to the other variety by listening. These grammar articles follow the usage of the Netherlands and point out Flemish variants where they matter.
