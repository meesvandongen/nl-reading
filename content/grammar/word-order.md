The most important rule of Dutch word order: in a main clause, the **conjugated verb is always in second place**. This is called *V2* (verb second).

## The verb in position 2

Position 1 is often the subject, but it doesn't have to be. It can also be a time, a place, an object or even a whole clause. Whatever comes first, the verb comes second, and if position 1 is **not** the subject, the subject moves to **after** the verb (inversion).

| 1 | 2 (verb) | subject | rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ik | ga | | morgen naar Utrecht. |
| Morgen | ga | ik | naar Utrecht. |
| Naar Utrecht | ga | ik | morgen. |

- Ik drink 's ochtends koffie. — I drink coffee in the morning.
- 's Ochtends drink ik koffie. — In the morning I drink coffee.
- In Nederland fietst iedereen. — In the Netherlands everyone cycles.
- Koffie drink ik niet, maar thee wel. — I don't drink coffee, but I do drink tea.
- Als het regent, blijf ik thuis. — If it rains, I stay at home.

Position 1 is one *element*, but that element can be long: in *Vorige week dinsdag om drie uur belde ze me op*, everything before *belde* is position 1.

Dutch speakers start sentences with something other than the subject very often, so you'll hear inversion all the time. The typical learner mistake is the English order: *Morgen ik ga* instead of *Morgen ga ik*.

Some words don't count for position 1: *en*, *maar*, *of*, *want* and words like *ja* and *nee*. After them, the order is normal.

- Ik blijf thuis, want ik ben ziek. — I'm staying at home, because I'm ill.
- Nee, ik heb geen tijd. — No, I don't have time.

## Time, manner, place

When a sentence has a time, a manner (how) and a place, the usual order is **time – manner – place**. English often does the opposite: place before time.

- Ik ga morgen met de trein naar Amsterdam. — I'm going to Amsterdam by train tomorrow.
- We fietsen zaterdag samen naar het strand. — We're cycling to the beach together on Saturday.

This isn't a strict rule (you can put the time first, for example), but it's the natural default.

## The verb bracket

When there is more than one verb, only the conjugated verb is in position 2. The other verb (an infinitive or a past participle) goes to the **very end** of the clause. Everything else goes in between, as if the two verbs were the two arms of a pair of tongs. Dutch grammar calls this the *tangconstructie*.

| 1 | 2 (verb) | middle | end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ik | wil | morgen naar Utrecht | gaan. |
| Ik | heb | gisteren een boek | gekocht. |
| Morgen | moet | ik vroeg | opstaan. |

- Ik wil vanavond een film kijken. — I want to watch a film tonight.
- We hebben gisteren lekker gegeten. — We had a nice meal yesterday.
- Kun je morgen om negen uur komen? — Can you come at nine tomorrow?
- Ze is vorig jaar naar Canada verhuisd. — She moved to Canada last year.

Separable verbs work the same way: the prefix goes to the end. *Ik bel je morgen op.* (See "Separable verbs".)

A common mistake is to keep the verbs together as in English: *Ik wil gaan naar Utrecht* should be *Ik wil naar Utrecht gaan*.

## Subordinate clauses: all verbs at the end

After words like *dat* (that), *omdat* (because), *als* (if, when), *of* (whether) and *terwijl* (while), the conjugated verb also goes to the end.

- Ik denk dat hij morgen komt. — I think he's coming tomorrow.
- Ik blijf thuis omdat ik ziek ben. — I'm staying at home because I'm ill.
- Ik weet niet of ze al gegeten heeft. — I don't know whether she's eaten yet.

Compare *want ik ben ziek* (normal order) with *omdat ik ziek ben* (verb at the end): both mean "because I'm ill". With two verbs at the end, both orders are correct: *omdat ik morgen moet werken* and *omdat ik morgen werken moet*. With a modal verb like *moeten*, the first is far more common.

## Questions

In yes/no questions the verb comes first: *Kom je morgen?* After a question word, the verb is second: *Wanneer kom je?* (See "Questions".)

## Summary

| clause type | where is the conjugated verb? |
| --- | --- |
| main clause | second element |
| yes/no question | first |
| subordinate clause | at the end |

Infinitives, participles and separable prefixes go to the end in all three.
