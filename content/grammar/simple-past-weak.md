The simple past (*onvoltooid verleden tijd*) is the Dutch "I worked, I lived". For regular (weak) verbs it is easy to make, and it uses the same *'t kofschip* rule as the past participle.

## The rule: stem + -te(n) or -de(n)

Take the stem (the *ik*-form) and add:

| stem ends in… | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| t, k, f, s, ch, p (*'t kofschip*, "soft ketchup") | -te | -ten |
| any other sound | -de | -den |

There is one form for all persons in the singular (*ik, jij, u, hij, zij, het*) and one for all persons in the plural (*wij, jullie, zij*).

| | werken | wonen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ik / jij / u / hij / zij | werkte | woonde |
| wij / jullie / zij | werkten | woonden |

- Ik werkte vroeger in een bakkerij. — I used to work in a bakery.
- Ze woonden tien jaar in Leuven. — They lived in Leuven for ten years.
- Hij fietste elke dag naar school. — He cycled to school every day.
- We speelden de hele middag buiten. — We played outside all afternoon.

## Spelling traps

**Check the infinitive, not the stem.** The stem of *leven* is *leef*, but the infinitive has a *v*, so it's *leefde*, not *leefte*. The same goes for *reizen → reisde*, *verhuizen → verhuisde*, *geloven → geloofde*.

**Stem ending in t or d: you write a double letter.** Nothing is dropped, so *praten → praatte*, *wachten → wachtte*, *zetten → zette*, *antwoorden → antwoordde*, *branden → brandde*. In speech you only hear one t or d, but in writing the double letter shows it's the past: *ik praat* (now) versus *ik praatte* (then).

**Vowels follow the normal spelling rules**: *maken → maakte*, *horen → hoorde*, *bellen → belde*.

- Het regende de hele dag. — It rained all day.
- We wachtten twintig minuten op de bus. — We waited twenty minutes for the bus.
- Ze antwoordde niet. — She didn't answer.
- Ik praatte nog even met de buurvrouw. — I chatted with the neighbour for a bit.
- Hij verhuisde in 2020 naar Rotterdam. — He moved to Rotterdam in 2020.
- Toen leefde mijn opa nog. — My grandad was still alive then.

## Separable verbs

As in the present tense, the prefix goes to the end of a main clause.

- Ze belde me gisteren op. — She rang me yesterday.
- We ruimden samen de keuken op. — We tidied the kitchen together.

## When does Dutch use the simple past?

In conversation, a single finished event is usually told in the perfect: *Ik heb gisteren een jas gekocht.* The simple past is used:

**In stories and writing.** Novels, fairy tales, news reports and anyone telling a story with one event after another use the simple past.

- Ze pakte haar tas, deed de deur dicht en liep naar het station. — She grabbed her bag, closed the door and walked to the station.

**For situations and habits in the past**, often with *vroeger* ("in the past, used to") or a *toen*-clause.

- Toen ik klein was, speelde ik elke dag buiten. — When I was little, I played outside every day.
- Vroeger woonden we in een dorp. — We used to live in a village.

**For states, with a handful of very common verbs**: *was/waren*, *had/hadden*, the modals (*kon, moest, wilde, mocht*) and *wist*, *dacht*, *vond*. Even in conversation these are normal in the simple past. Most of them are irregular; see the article on strong and irregular verbs.

- Het was een mooie dag. — It was a beautiful day.
- Ik had geen tijd. — I didn't have time.
- Ik wist het niet. — I didn't know.

A typical spoken pattern: the event in the perfect, the background in the simple past.

- We zijn gisteren naar het strand gegaan, maar het regende. — We went to the beach yesterday, but it was raining.

In Flanders (and the south of the Netherlands), speakers use the perfect even more in conversation than people in the north.

## Common mistakes

Writing *-te* where *-de* belongs (*leefte*, *reiste* for "travelled": correct is *reisde*), forgetting the *-n* in the plural (*wij werkte*), and telling a one-off event in conversation in the simple past. *Gisteren kookte ik pasta* isn't wrong, but it sounds like the start of a story; *Gisteren heb ik pasta gekookt* is what people normally say.
