A relative clause (*betrekkelijke bijzin*) tells you more about a noun: "the man **who lives next door**", "the book **that I'm reading**". In Dutch it starts with a relative pronoun, and like every subordinate clause, it has **the verb at the end**.

## die and dat

The choice depends on the noun:

| noun | relative pronoun |
| --- | --- |
| de-word, singular | die |
| het-word, singular | dat |
| all plurals | die |

It doesn't matter whether the noun is a person or a thing, or whether it's the subject or the object of the clause.

- De man die daar staat, is mijn buurman. — The man standing over there is my neighbour.
- De fiets die ik gisteren heb gekocht, is al kapot. — The bike I bought yesterday is already broken.
- Het boek dat ik lees, is erg spannend. — The book I'm reading is really exciting.
- De kinderen die buiten spelen, zijn van de buren. — The children playing outside are the neighbours'.
- Ken je het meisje dat naast Sanne zit? — Do you know the girl sitting next to Sanne?
- Het huisje dat we hebben gehuurd, ligt vlak bij zee. — The cottage we rented is right by the sea.

Watch out for *het meisje dat*: *meisje* is a het-word, like all diminutives (*het huisje*), so it takes *dat*, even though a girl is a person.

English can leave out the pronoun ("the book I'm reading"); Dutch can't. *Het boek ik lees* is wrong.

## waar + preposition

When the relative clause needs a preposition, and the noun is a thing, Dutch uses *waar* + preposition, usually written as one word: *waarin* (in which), *waarover* (about which), *waarmee* (with which; *met* becomes *mee*). For a place, plain *waar* is enough.

- Dit is het huis waarin ik ben geboren. — This is the house I was born in.
- De stad waar ik woon, is niet zo groot. — The town where I live isn't very big.
- De pen waarmee ik schrijf, is van mijn opa. — The pen I'm writing with is my grandad's.
- Dat is het probleem waarover we gisteren spraken. — That's the problem we talked about yesterday.

In speech, *waar* and the preposition are often split, with the preposition just before the verbs at the end:

- Het onderwerp waar ik het over wil hebben, is klimaatverandering. — The subject I want to talk about is climate change.

*Het huis in dat ik woon* is wrong: after a preposition, *die* and *dat* turn into *waar*.

## preposition + wie for people

For people, you can use preposition + *wie*.

- De man met wie ik praatte, is mijn leraar. — The man I was talking to is my teacher.
- De vrouw aan wie ik het boek gaf, woont in Gent. — The woman I gave the book to lives in Ghent.
- Is dat de collega over wie je vertelde? — Is that the colleague you were telling me about?

*Waar* + preposition is also common for people in speech (*de man waarmee ik praatte*), but preposition + *wie* is more careful.

## wat

Use *wat* after *alles* (everything), *iets* (something), *niets* (nothing), *veel* and *weinig*, after a superlative (*het mooiste*), and when the clause refers to a whole sentence.

- Alles wat je zegt, is waar. — Everything you say is true.
- Is er iets wat ik kan doen? — Is there anything I can do?
- Dat is het mooiste wat ik ooit heb gezien. — That's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen.
- Hij kwam weer te laat, wat ik erg vervelend vond. — He was late again, which I found really annoying.

After *iets*, *dat* is also correct: *iets dat ik kan doen*.

In speech you'll often hear *wat* after a het-word (*het boek wat ik lees*), but standard Dutch uses *dat* there.

A clause with *wie* or *wat* without a noun before it means "whoever" or "whatever":

- Wie het laatst lacht, lacht het best. — He who laughs last laughs best.

## Word order

All the verbs go to the end of the relative clause. If the relative clause belongs to the subject, the main verb comes right after it, often after a comma: *De fiets die ik heb gekocht, is kapot.*

The relative clause can also move to after the verbs at the end of the main clause, which sounds very natural:

- Ik heb een boek gekocht dat over Amsterdam gaat. — I bought a book about Amsterdam.

## Common mistakes

Using *die* with het-words (*het meisje die*), using *wie* or *wat* for ordinary nouns (*de man wie*, *het boek wat*), leaving out the pronoun, and forgetting to move the verb to the end (*de man die woont naast ons* should be *de man die naast ons woont*).
