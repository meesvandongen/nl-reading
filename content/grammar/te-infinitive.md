Dutch *te* + infinitive is like English "to" + verb: *te slapen* is "to sleep". But not every verb takes *te*, and "in order to" has its own construction with *om … te*. The infinitive always goes to the end of the clause.

## Verbs followed by te

Many verbs are followed by *te* + infinitive. Common ones: *proberen* (to try), *beginnen* (to begin), *vergeten* (to forget), *hoeven* (to need to), *durven* (to dare), *besluiten* (to decide), *beloven* (to promise), *hopen* (to hope), *weigeren* (to refuse), *van plan zijn* (to be planning to).

- Ik probeer elke dag Nederlands te lezen. — I try to read Dutch every day.
- Het begint te regenen. — It's starting to rain.
- Vergeet niet de deur op slot te doen. — Don't forget to lock the door.
- Hij durft niet alleen te reizen. — He doesn't dare to travel alone.
- We hebben besloten te verhuizen. — We've decided to move.
- Ze belooft op tijd te zijn. — She promises to be on time.

**hoeven** is used only with a negative word (*niet, geen, nooit*) or with *alleen* or *maar*. It's the negative of *moeten*: "don't have to".

- Je hoeft niet te komen. — You don't have to come.
- Je hoeft alleen maar te bellen. — All you have to do is call.

*Ik moet niet werken* means something different: "I mustn't work" or "I shouldn't work". For "I don't have to work", say *Ik hoef niet te werken*.

In casual speech you'll hear *hoeven* and *durven* without *te* (*Je hoeft niet komen*), but write it with *te*.

## Separable verbs: te in the middle

With a separable verb, *te* goes between the prefix and the verb, written as three separate words: *op te bellen*, *mee te nemen*, *af te wassen*, *uit te nodigen*.

- Vergeet niet je moeder op te bellen. — Don't forget to call your mother.
- Probeer morgen op tijd op te staan. — Try to get up on time tomorrow.
- Ik heb geen zin om af te wassen. — I don't feel like doing the dishes.

*Te opbellen* is wrong.

## om … te: "in order to"

To give a purpose, use *om … te*. *Om* starts the phrase, everything else comes in between, and *te* + infinitive comes last.

- Ik ga naar de bakker om brood te kopen. — I'm going to the baker's to buy bread.
- We vertrekken vroeg om de file te vermijden. — We're leaving early to avoid the traffic jam.
- Ze leert Nederlands om hier te kunnen werken. — She's learning Dutch to be able to work here.

*Om … te* is also used after nouns and adjectives, and after *het is … om*:

- Ik heb geen tijd om te koken. — I don't have time to cook.
- Heb je zin om mee te gaan? — Do you feel like coming along?
- Het is leuk om nieuwe mensen te ontmoeten. — It's nice to meet new people.
- Dit boek is te moeilijk om te lezen. — This book is too difficult to read.

After many of the verbs in the first section, *om* is optional, especially in longer sentences: *We hebben besloten (om) te verhuizen.*

Don't use *voor* here: *voor brood te kopen* is a common mistake. (In Flanders you'll hear it in casual speech, but it isn't standard.)

Other prepositions combine with *te* too, as in *zonder te* (without …ing):

- Hij stak over zonder te kijken. — He crossed without looking.

## Verbs without te

These verbs take a bare infinitive, with no *te*:

| verbs | example |
| --- | --- |
| modals: kunnen, moeten, mogen, willen, zullen | Ik kan niet zwemmen. |
| gaan, komen | We gaan fietsen. |
| laten | Laat me even nadenken. |
| zien, horen | Ik zag hem vallen. |
| blijven | Blijf je eten? |
| leren, helpen (usually) | Ik leer autorijden. |

- Ik kan niet zwemmen. — I can't swim.
- We gaan morgen fietsen. — We're going cycling tomorrow.
- Kom je bij ons eten? — Are you coming to eat with us?
- Laat me even nadenken. — Let me think for a moment.
- Ik hoor de buren zingen. — I can hear the neighbours singing.
- Blijf je eten? — Are you staying for dinner?

*Ik wil te gaan* and *Ik ga te koken* are typical mistakes. *Zitten, staan, liggen* and *lopen* do take *te*, in sentences like *Hij zit te lezen*; see the article on *aan het* and *zitten te*.
