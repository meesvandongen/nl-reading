The Dutch present tense is simple: there are only three forms for most verbs. You build them from the **stem**.

## Finding the stem

Take the infinitive and remove *-en*: *werken → werk*, *drinken → drink*. Then apply the spelling rules (see "Pronunciation and spelling"), because the stem is now a closed syllable:

| rule | infinitive | stem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| long vowel: write it double | lopen, maken, eten | loop, maak, eet |
| double consonant: write it single | zitten, bakken, liggen | zit, bak, lig |
| v becomes f, z becomes s | leven, schrijven, lezen | leef, schrijf, lees |
| one-syllable verbs: remove the -n (aa becomes a) | gaan, staan, doen, zien | ga, sta, doe, zie |

Dutch words never end in *v* or *z*, which is why *schrijven* becomes *schrijf*.

## The endings

| | werken | lopen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ik | werk | loop |
| jij / je | werkt | loopt |
| u | werkt | loopt |
| hij / zij / het | werkt | loopt |
| wij / we | werken | lopen |
| jullie | werken | lopen |
| zij / ze | werken | lopen |

So: *ik* gets the stem, *jij*, *u* and *hij/zij/het* get stem + *t*, and all plurals use the infinitive.

- Ik werk in Rotterdam. — I work in Rotterdam.
- Hij leest de krant. — He's reading the newspaper.
- We eten om zes uur. — We eat at six.
- Jullie spreken goed Nederlands. — You speak Dutch well.
- Ze woont al tien jaar in Gent. — She has been living in Ghent for ten years.

## jij after the verb: no -t

When *jij* or *je* comes **after** the verb (in a question, or when something else starts the sentence), the *-t* disappears.

- Werk jij morgen? — Are you working tomorrow?
- Loop je mee? — Are you coming along?
- Wanneer kom je? — When are you coming?
- Morgen werk je thuis, toch? — You're working from home tomorrow, right?

This only happens with *jij/je*. With *u*, *hij* and *zij*, the *-t* stays: *Komt u ook?*, *Werkt hij morgen?* Be careful when *je* means "your": in *Werkt je vader hier?* (Does your father work here?), the subject is *je vader*, so the *-t* stays.

## Stems ending in -t or -d

If the stem already ends in *-t*, you don't add another one: *ik zit, jij zit, hij zit*; *ik weet, hij weet*.

If the stem ends in *-d*, you **do** add the *-t*, even though you don't hear it: *ik word, jij wordt, hij wordt*; *ik vind, hij vindt*. This is a classic spelling mistake, also for Dutch people. Without the *-t* in inversion: *Word je moe?*, *Vind jij dat ook?*

- Hij zit op de bank. — He's sitting on the sofa.
- Wat vindt u ervan? — What do you think of it?
- Het wordt koud. — It's getting cold.
- Vind jij dat ook? — Do you think so too?

## Irregular verbs

A few very common verbs are irregular.

| | zijn | hebben | kunnen | willen | zullen | mogen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ik | ben | heb | kan | wil | zal | mag |
| jij / je | bent | hebt | kunt / kan | wilt / wil | zult / zal | mag |
| u | bent | hebt / heeft | kunt / kan | wilt / wil | zult / zal | mag |
| hij / zij / het | is | heeft | kan | wil | zal | mag |
| wij / jullie / zij | zijn | hebben | kunnen | willen | zullen | mogen |

Note that *hij kan*, *hij wil*, *hij zal* and *hij mag* have no *-t*. After the verb, *jij* again loses the *-t*: *ben jij*, *heb jij*, *kun jij*, *wil jij*.

- Ik ben moe. — I'm tired.
- Heb je een fiets? — Do you have a bike?
- Kan hij zwemmen? — Can he swim?

## One tense, many meanings

The Dutch present covers the English simple present ("I work"), the continuous ("I'm working") and often the future ("I'll call you tomorrow"). To stress that something is happening right now, use *aan het* + infinitive.

- Ik bel je morgen. — I'll call you tomorrow.
- Ik ben aan het koken. — I'm cooking (right now).

## Common mistakes

Writing *ik wordt* (it's *ik word*) or *hij word* (it's *hij wordt*). Keeping the *-t* in questions: *Werkt jij?* should be *Werk jij?* Adding a *-t* to *kan*, *wil*, *zal*, *mag*. And translating "do" in questions: "Do you work?" is simply *Werk je?*
