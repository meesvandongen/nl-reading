Modal verbs say whether you can, must, want to or may do something. In Dutch they are *kunnen*, *moeten*, *willen*, *mogen* and *zullen*.

## Conjugation

| | kunnen | moeten | willen | mogen | zullen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ik | kan | moet | wil | mag | zal |
| jij / je | kunt / kan | moet | wilt / wil | mag | zult / zal |
| u | kunt / kan | moet | wilt / wil | mag | zult / zal |
| hij / zij / het | kan | moet | wil | mag | zal |
| wij / jullie / zij | kunnen | moeten | willen | mogen | zullen |
| past | kon, konden | moest, moesten | wilde, wilden | mocht, mochten | zou, zouden |

*hij kan*, *hij wil*, *hij mag* and *hij zal* have no *-t*. For *jij*, both forms are fine (*jij kunt* is a bit more formal than *jij kan*). In questions: *Kun je…?* or *Kan je…?*, *Wil je…?*, *Zul je…?* In speech you'll also hear *wou* for *wilde*.

## The infinitive goes to the end

The modal verb is conjugated and stands in second place; the main verb is an infinitive and goes to the **end** of the clause.

- Ik kan goed zwemmen. — I can swim well.
- Je moet om acht uur op school zijn. — You have to be at school at eight.
- Wil je iets drinken? — Would you like something to drink?
- Mag ik hier fietsen? — Am I allowed to cycle here?
- Zullen we morgen afspreken? — Shall we meet up tomorrow?
- Ik kan morgen niet komen. — I can't come tomorrow.

Not *Ik kan komen niet morgen*: everything goes between the modal and the infinitive.

## What they mean

| verb | meaning | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kunnen | can, to be able to; to be possible | *Dat kan.* (That's possible, that's fine.) |
| moeten | must, to have to; should (advice) | *Je moet die film zien!* (You should see that film!) |
| willen | to want | *Ik wil naar huis.* (I want to go home.) |
| mogen | may, to be allowed to; to like (a person) | *Ik mag hem wel.* (I quite like him.) |
| zullen | will (promise, future); shall (suggestion) | *Ik zal het doen.* (I'll do it, I promise.) |

Watch out: *willen* looks like English "will", but it means "want". *Ik wil komen* is "I want to come". For the future, Dutch mostly uses the present tense (*Ik kom morgen*) or *gaan* (*Ik ga morgen werken*). *Zullen* is used for promises, suggestions (*Zullen we gaan?*, Shall we go?) and formal predictions.

The past form *zou* means "would" and makes polite requests.

- Zou je het raam dicht willen doen? — Would you mind closing the window?
- Ik zou graag een kamer willen reserveren. — I'd like to book a room.

## Without an infinitive

When the meaning is clear, the infinitive is often left out, especially verbs of movement (*gaan*), *doen* and *hebben*.

- Ik moet nu naar huis. — I have to go home now.
- Ik wil een ijsje. — I want an ice cream.
- Dat mag niet. — That's not allowed.
- Ik kan het niet. — I can't do it.

## moeten and "must": niet hoeven

This is the big trap for English speakers. "You don't have to come" is **not** *Je moet niet komen*. That means "You shouldn't come" or "Don't come". For "not have to", Dutch uses **niet hoeven + te**.

*hoeven* is only used with a negative word (*niet*, *geen*, *nooit*, *alleen*), and it needs *te* before the infinitive: *ik hoef, jij hoeft, hij hoeft, wij hoeven*.

- Je hoeft niet te komen. — You don't have to come.
- Je hoeft geen jas mee te nemen. — You don't need to bring a coat.
- Hoef je morgen niet te werken? — Don't you have to work tomorrow?
- Je moet niet zo hard werken. — You shouldn't work so hard.
- Je mag hier niet parkeren. — You mustn't park here.

| English | Dutch |
| --- | --- |
| must, have to | moeten |
| don't have to, needn't | niet hoeven te |
| mustn't, not allowed to | niet mogen |
| should | moeten, zou moeten |

In speech you'll hear *hoeven* without *te* (*Je hoeft niet komen*), but in writing, keep the *te*.

## In the perfect tense

In the perfect tense, the modal stays an infinitive, so you get two infinitives at the end.

- Ik heb vannacht niet kunnen slapen. — I couldn't sleep last night.
