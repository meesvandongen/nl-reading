Dutch pronouns work much like English ones, with one big difference: many of them have two forms, a **stressed** (full) form and an **unstressed** (short) form. In normal speech and writing, the short form is the default.

## Subject pronouns

| | stressed | unstressed | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st singular | ik | — | I |
| 2nd singular, informal | jij | je | you |
| 2nd singular, formal | u | — | you |
| 3rd singular | hij | — | he |
| | zij | ze | she |
| | het | — | it |
| 1st plural | wij | we | we |
| 2nd plural | jullie | — | you (plural) |
| 3rd plural | zij | ze | they |

In speech, *ik*, *hij* and *het* also get shortened (*'k*, *ie*, *'t*), and you'll see that in informal chats and in dialogue in books: *Heeft ie gebeld?* In normal writing, use the full forms.

## Object pronouns

| stressed | unstressed | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mij | me | me |
| jou | je | you |
| u | — | you (formal) |
| hem | — | him, it |
| haar | — | her |
| het | — | it |
| ons | — | us |
| jullie | — | you (plural) |
| hen / hun | ze | them |

## Short forms are the default

Use *je*, *ze*, *we* and *me* unless you want to stress the pronoun.

- Ik zie je morgen. — See you tomorrow.
- We gaan naar huis. — We're going home.
- Ze woont in Utrecht. — She lives in Utrecht.
- Kun je me helpen? — Can you help me?
- Heb je hem gezien? — Have you seen him?

Use the stressed forms for **contrast or emphasis**, and when the pronoun stands on its own.

- Jij bent moe, maar ik niet. — You're tired, but I'm not.
- Ik bel jou, niet hem. — I'm calling you, not him.
- Wie wil er koffie? Wij! — Who wants coffee? We do!

After a preposition both forms are used: *voor mij* and *voor me* both mean "for me", *met jou* or *met je* "with you".

- Is dit voor mij? — Is this for me?

Saying *Ik zie jou morgen* isn't wrong, but it stresses the *you*: "I'll see *you* tomorrow" (and not someone else).

## ze: she or they?

*ze* can mean "she" or "they" (and as an object "them"). The verb usually tells you which: singular *werkt*, plural *werken*.

- Ze werkt in een winkel. — She works in a shop.
- Ze werken in een winkel. — They work in a shop.
- Ik heb ze gisteren gezien. — I saw them yesterday.

For "them", *ze* is by far the most common. The full forms *hen* and *hun* are only used for people. Officially *hen* is the direct object and comes after prepositions, and *hun* is the indirect object (*Ik geef hun een boek*), but many Dutch speakers mix them up. One thing to avoid: *hun* as a subject (*Hun hebben gelijk*). You'll hear it a lot in the Netherlands, but it is considered incorrect. Say *Zij hebben gelijk* or *Ze hebben gelijk*.

## hij and het for things

English uses "it" for all things. Dutch uses **hij/hem for de-words** and **het for het-words**. For plural things, use *ze*.

- Ik zoek mijn sleutel, maar ik kan hem niet vinden. — I'm looking for my key, but I can't find it.
- Het boek is mooi, maar het is duur. — The book is beautiful, but it's expensive.
- De appels zijn lekker, ik heb ze net gekocht. — The apples are tasty, I just bought them.

Learners often say *het* for every thing; Dutch speakers will say *hij*: *De trein? Hij is te laat.* In Flanders, *zij/ze* is also used for some de-words that are traditionally feminine, and for abstract words like *de regering* (the government) you'll hear *ze* in the Netherlands too.

## je for "you" in general

Like English "you", *je* is also used for people in general.

- Je weet maar nooit. — You never know.
- In Nederland kun je overal fietsen. — In the Netherlands you can cycle everywhere.

## Common mistakes

Using stressed forms everywhere sounds insistent. Using a short form alone or with stress doesn't work: the answer to *Wie?* is *Ik!* or *Jij!*, never *Je!* Don't confuse *jou* (you, object) with *jouw* (your). And remember that *je* can also mean "your": *Waar is je jas?* (Where is your coat?) For the choice between *u* and *jij*, see the article "u or jij?".
