A subordinate clause (*bijzin*) is a clause that starts with a word like *dat* (that), *omdat* (because) or *als* (if). It can't stand on its own. In Dutch it has its own word order: **the conjugated verb goes to the end**.

## The verb at the end

Compare a main clause and a subordinate clause:

- Het regent. — It's raining.
- Ik blijf thuis omdat het regent. — I'm staying at home because it's raining.
- Je bent moe. — You're tired.
- Ik weet dat je moe bent. — I know you're tired.
- Ik denk dat hij morgen komt. — I think he's coming tomorrow.

In English you can leave out "that"; in Dutch you can't. *Ik denk hij komt* is wrong.

## All the verbs at the end

If there's more than one verb, they all go to the end together, as a cluster. *Niet*, objects and time words come before them. A separable verb is written as one word again.

- Ze zegt dat ze morgen niet kan komen. — She says she can't come tomorrow.
- Ik weet niet of hij het boek al heeft gelezen. — I don't know whether he's read the book yet.
- Bel me even als je aankomt. — Give me a call when you arrive.

With *heeft gelezen* you may also hear *gelezen heeft*; both are correct. The article on verb clusters goes into the details.

## The most common conjunctions

| conjunction | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| dat | that |
| omdat | because |
| als | if; when (present, future, whenever) |
| wanneer | when |
| toen | when (one moment in the past) |
| terwijl | while; whereas |
| voordat | before |
| nadat | after |
| hoewel | although |
| of | whether, if |
| zodat | so that |
| totdat | until |

- Ik leer Nederlands omdat mijn vriendin uit Utrecht komt. — I'm learning Dutch because my girlfriend is from Utrecht.
- Terwijl ik kookte, ging de telefoon. — While I was cooking, the phone rang.
- Was je handen voordat je gaat eten. — Wash your hands before you eat.
- Nadat we hadden gegeten, gingen we wandelen. — After we'd eaten, we went for a walk.
- Hoewel het koud was, zaten we buiten. — Although it was cold, we sat outside.
- Weet jij of de winkel vandaag open is? — Do you know whether the shop is open today?
- Ik praat langzaam, zodat iedereen me begrijpt. — I'm speaking slowly so that everyone understands me.
- Wacht hier totdat ik terugkom. — Wait here until I get back.

In speech you'll often hear the short forms *voor* (for *voordat*) and *tot* (for *totdat*).

When "if" means "whether", use *of*, not *als*: *Ik weet niet of hij komt.*

## Subordinate clause first: verb, verb

When the sentence starts with the subordinate clause, the whole clause counts as the first element. The main clause then starts with its verb, so you get two verbs side by side, separated by a comma.

- Als het regent, blijf ik thuis. — If it rains, I stay at home.
- Omdat ik ziek was, ben ik niet gekomen. — Because I was ill, I didn't come.
- Toen ik in Gent woonde, fietste ik overal naartoe. — When I lived in Ghent, I cycled everywhere.

*Als het regent, ik blijf thuis* is a very common mistake. The subject goes after the verb: *blijf ik*.

## als, toen or wanneer?

English "when" has three Dutch translations.

| | use it for | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *toen* | one occasion or period in the past | *toen ik jong was* |
| *als* | the present and future; repeated events, also in the past ("whenever"); and "if" | *als je komt*, *als ik bij oma was* |
| *wanneer* | like *als* in time meaning, a bit more formal; also the question word "when?" | *wanneer de bel gaat* |

- Toen ik jong was, woonde ik in Zeeland. — When I was young, I lived in Zeeland.
- Als je tijd hebt, kom dan even langs. — If you've got time, drop by.
- Als ik bij oma was, kreeg ik altijd een koekje. — Whenever I was at Grandma's, I always got a biscuit.
- Wanneer de bel gaat, mogen de kinderen naar buiten. — When the bell rings, the children can go outside.
- Wanneer kom je? — When are you coming?

A frequent mistake is *als* for a single moment in the past: *Als ik in Gent aankwam…* should be *Toen ik in Gent aankwam…*

Careful: *toen* is also an ordinary word meaning "then" (at that time). Then it's not a conjunction and the verb comes straight after it:

- Toen ging ik naar huis. — Then I went home.

The words *en, maar, of, want* and *dus* join two main clauses and don't move the verb; see the article on coordinating conjunctions.
