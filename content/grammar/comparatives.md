To compare things, Dutch adds **-er** (bigger) and **-st** (biggest), just like English. The difference is that Dutch does this with almost all adjectives, also long ones.

## The forms

| basic | comparative | superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| klein (small) | kleiner | kleinst |
| mooi (beautiful) | mooier | mooist |
| groot (big) | groter | grootst |
| dik (thick, fat) | dikker | dikst |
| lief (sweet) | liever | liefst |
| vies (dirty) | viezer | viest |
| duur (expensive) | duurder | duurst |

The comparative follows the usual spelling rules, because *-er* starts a new syllable: *groot → groter*, *dik → dikker*, *lief → liever*, *vies → viezer*. The superlative doesn't change the spelling: *grootst*, *dikst*, *liefst*. Adjectives that end in *-s* only add *-t*: *viest*.

After **r**, the comparative is **-der**: *duur → duurder*, *zwaar → zwaarder* (heavier), *ver → verder* (further), *lekker → lekkerder* (tastier).

## Comparing with dan

"Than" is **dan**. "As … as" is **even … als** or **zo … als**.

- Utrecht is kleiner dan Amsterdam. — Utrecht is smaller than Amsterdam.
- Mijn broer is ouder dan ik. — My brother is older than me.
- Een fiets is goedkoper dan een auto. — A bike is cheaper than a car.
- Ze is even oud als ik. — She's as old as me.
- Het is vandaag niet zo koud als gisteren. — It's not as cold today as yesterday.

In everyday speech, many Dutch speakers say *groter als* instead of *groter dan*. You'll hear it all the time, but it's considered incorrect, so use *dan*, certainly in writing. Officially it's *dan ik* (subject form), not *dan mij*, although *dan mij* is very common in speech.

## Irregular forms

| basic | comparative | superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| goed (good) | beter | best |
| veel (much, many) | meer | meest |
| weinig (little, few) | minder | minst |
| graag (gladly) | liever | liefst |

*graag*, *liever*, *liefst* is how Dutch says "like", "prefer", "like most" with a verb.

- Mijn Nederlands wordt steeds beter. — My Dutch is getting better and better.
- Ik drink liever thee dan koffie. — I prefer tea to coffee.
- Ik heb minder tijd dan vroeger. — I have less time than I used to.
- Wie heeft de meeste punten? — Who has the most points?
- De meeste mensen gaan met de fiets. — Most people go by bike.

## meer and meest

Most long adjectives still take *-er* and *-st*: *interessanter*, *moeilijker*, *gevaarlijker*. But with past participles, some long words and adjectives ending in *-isch*, *meer* and *meest* often sound better.

- Hij is meer ontspannen dan vroeger. — He's more relaxed than he used to be.
- Dat is de meest logische oplossing. — That's the most logical solution.

## The superlative: het mooist(e)

Before a noun, the superlative gets the article of the noun and the ending *-e*: *de mooiste stad*, *het grootste huis*.

- Amsterdam is de grootste stad van Nederland. — Amsterdam is the biggest city in the Netherlands.
- Wat is het hoogste gebouw van België? — What's the tallest building in Belgium?

After a verb, or as an adverb, use **het** + superlative, with or without *-e*: *het mooist* or *het mooiste*. Both are correct.

- In de lente is het park het mooist. — The park is at its most beautiful in spring.
- Wie rent het snelst? — Who runs the fastest?
- Dit vind ik het lekkerste. — I think this one is the tastiest.

Comparatives before a noun follow the normal adjective rule: *een grotere stad*, but *een groter huis*.

## Useful patterns

- Het wordt steeds kouder. — It's getting colder and colder.
- Hoe eerder, hoe beter. — The sooner, the better.

## Common mistakes

Using *meer* where Dutch uses *-er*: *meer groot* should be *groter*, and *meer interessant* is usually *interessanter*. Forgetting the *d* after *r*: *duurer* should be *duurder*. Using *als* for "than". And *het meest* before a noun: "most people" is *de meeste mensen*.
