English has one definite article, "the". Dutch has two: *de* and *het*. About two thirds of all nouns are **de-words** and one third are **het-words**. The indefinite article *een* (a, an) is the same for both, and is pronounced with a weak vowel, like "un".

| | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| de-word | de man | de mannen |
| het-word | het huis | de huizen |
| indefinite | een man, een huis | mannen, huizen |

- De vrouw leest het boek. — The woman is reading the book.
- Het kind speelt met de hond. — The child is playing with the dog.
- De kinderen spelen buiten. — The children are playing outside.

For most nouns there is no logic: it's *de tafel* but *het bed*, *de stoel* but *het raam*. Even native speakers simply know it. The best habit is to **always learn a noun together with its article**: not *huis*, but *het huis*.

## Rules that help

A few rules do work, and they cover a lot of words.

| always **het** | examples |
| --- | --- |
| diminutives (-je) | *het meisje*, *het tafeltje* (but *de tafel*) |
| infinitives used as nouns | *het eten* (food), *het leven* (life), *het zwemmen* |
| words ending in -ment | *het moment*, *het document*, *het appartement* |
| words ending in -um | *het museum*, *het centrum*, *het album* |
| words ending in -isme | *het toerisme*, *het racisme* |
| languages and metals | *het Nederlands*, *het goud* |

| always **de** | examples |
| --- | --- |
| all plurals | *de huizen*, *de meisjes* |
| words ending in -ing | *de woning*, *de oefening* |
| words ending in -heid | *de vrijheid*, *de waarheid* |
| words ending in -tie | *de informatie*, *de politie* |
| words ending in -teit | *de universiteit*, *de kwaliteit* |
| most words for people and jobs | *de man*, *de vrouw*, *de bakker*, *de leraar* |

Exceptions exist: it is *de datum* (date), and for people *het kind* (child) and *het meisje* (girl, a diminutive). Many nouns starting with *ge-* are het-words (*het gesprek*, *het gebouw*, *het geluid*), but not all of them (*de gedachte*).

**Compounds** take the article of their last part:

- het huis, het ziekenhuis — the house, the hospital
- de kamer, de slaapkamer — the room, the bedroom
- het boek, het woordenboek — the book, the dictionary
- de fiets, de bakfiets — the bike, the cargo bike

## Why it matters

The article isn't only about *de* or *het*. It decides several other words in the sentence:

| | de-word | het-word |
| --- | --- | --- |
| this | *deze fiets* | *dit huis* |
| that | *die fiets* | *dat huis* |
| who/which (relative) | *de fiets die…* | *het huis dat…* |
| een + adjective | *een nieuwe fiets* | *een nieuw huis* |
| "it" | *hij* | *het* |

- Deze fiets is nieuw. — This bike is new.
- Dit huis is te klein. — This house is too small.
- Ik heb een nieuwe fiets. — I have a new bike.
- Ik heb een nieuw huis. — I have a new house.
- De man die daar loopt, is mijn buurman. — The man walking over there is my neighbour.
- Het meisje dat daar staat, is mijn zus. — The girl standing over there is my sister.

## Tips and common mistakes

If you have to guess, guess *de*: you'll be right two times out of three. Don't forget that plurals are always *de*, even for het-words: *het boek*, *de boeken*. And diminutives are always *het*, even when the base word is a de-word: *de tafel*, *het tafeltje*.

A few words have both articles with different meanings: *de bal* (ball) and *het bal* (a ball, the dance), *de blik* (look, glance) and *het blik* (tin, can). A few others differ between countries: *het moment* is standard, but in Flanders you'll often hear *de moment*.

Don't worry if you get it wrong: everyone will still understand you. Dutch people notice it, but it never blocks communication, and it's the one thing even very advanced learners keep working on.
