*Er* is one of the most frequent words in Dutch, and one of the hardest for learners, because it does several different jobs. It's always unstressed, and when it doesn't start the sentence, it usually comes early, right after the verb. Here are its four main uses, plus one in the passive.

## 1. er = "there" (a place)

*Er* refers to a place that has already been mentioned. It's the unstressed version of *daar*.

- Ik ken Leiden goed, want ik woon er al jaren. — I know Leiden well; I've lived there for years.
- Ben je er al eens geweest? — Have you ever been there?
- Ik ga er morgen naartoe. — I'm going there tomorrow.

In this meaning, *er* can't start a sentence. If you want to put the place first, or stress it, use *daar*:

- Daar woon ik al jaren. — That's where I've lived for years.

## 2. er with a number or quantity

When you use a number or a word like *veel, weinig, een paar, genoeg, geen* or *hoeveel* without the noun, you need *er*. English says "of them", "one", or nothing at all. If someone asks *Hoeveel kinderen heb je?*, you answer:

- Ik heb er twee. — I've got two.
- Ik wil er nog een. — I'd like another one.
- Ik heb er geen. — I haven't got any.
- Hoeveel heb je er gekocht? — How many did you buy?

Saying just *Ik heb twee* sounds incomplete to Dutch ears. This *er* is the one learners forget most often.

## 3. er as a dummy subject ("there is / there are")

When the subject is new or indefinite (*een*, *geen*, a plural without *de*, *iemand*, *iets*, *niemand*, *veel*…), Dutch starts with *er*. The verb agrees with the real subject.

- Er is een probleem. — There's a problem.
- Er zijn geen eieren meer. — There are no eggs left.
- Er wonen veel studenten in deze straat. — Lots of students live in this street.
- Is er nog koffie? — Is there any coffee left?
- Wat is er? — What's wrong?
- Ik denk dat er iemand thuis is. — I think someone's home.

Dutch often uses a verb of position instead of *zijn*: *staan* (standing, upright), *liggen* (lying flat), *zitten* (inside something), *hangen*.

- Er staat een man voor de deur. — There's a man at the door.
- Er ligt een brief op tafel. — There's a letter on the table.
- Er zit geen suiker in. — There's no sugar in it.

With a definite subject (*de man*, *mijn fiets*), there's no *er*: *De man staat voor de deur.* If a place starts the sentence, *er* is usually left out in the Netherlands (*Op tafel ligt een brief*); in Flanders you'll often hear it anyway (*Op tafel ligt er een brief*).

Don't translate "there is" as *daar is*. *Daar is een probleem* means "There, look, is a problem."

## 4. er + preposition

After a preposition, Dutch doesn't use *het* for a thing. Instead, *er* and the preposition join together: not *over het* but *erover*, not *met het* but *ermee*. In a sentence they're often split. The article on *erop, daarmee, waarover* covers this in more detail.

- Ik denk erover na. — I'm thinking about it.
- Ik heb er geen zin in. — I don't feel like it.
- Hij is er erg blij mee. — He's very happy with it.
- Ik heb er gisteren over gelezen. — I read about it yesterday.

## 5. er in the passive

In a passive sentence with no subject, or with an indefinite one, *er* fills the first position.

- Er wordt gedanst en gezongen. — There's dancing and singing.
- Er wordt aangebeld. — Someone's ringing the doorbell.
- Er wordt een nieuwe brug gebouwd. — A new bridge is being built.

## Two jobs, one er (or two)

Sometimes one *er* does two jobs at once. And sometimes you need two:

- Ik heb er twee van gegeten. — I ate two of them.
- Er zijn er nog drie. — There are three left.

In the first sentence, *er* is both the quantity *er* and the *er* of *ervan*. In the second, the first *er* is the dummy subject and the second goes with the number.

## Tips

When you read, ask yourself which *er* you're looking at: is there a place, a number, an indefinite subject, a preposition, or a passive? And when you speak, check the two you're most likely to forget: *er* with numbers, and *er* with "there is".
