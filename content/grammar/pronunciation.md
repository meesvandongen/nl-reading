Dutch spelling looks strange at first, but it is very regular. Once you know how vowel length is written and a handful of letter combinations, you can pronounce almost any word you read.

## Short and long vowels

Every Dutch vowel comes in a short and a long version, and the difference changes the meaning: *man* (man) and *maan* (moon) are different words.

| short | example | long | example |
| ----- | ------- | ---- | ------- |
| a | *man* (man) | aa | *maan* (moon) |
| e | *bed* (bed) | ee | *been* (leg) |
| i | *vis* (fish) | ie | *vier* (four) |
| o | *bot* (bone) | oo | *boot* (boat) |
| u | *bus* (bus) | uu | *muur* (wall) |

Short *a* is pronounced at the back of the mouth, closer to the *a* in "father" than in "cat". Long *aa* is the "father" sound, but longer. *ee* is like "ay" in "day" and *oo* like "oa" in British "boat". *uu* doesn't exist in English: say "ee" with rounded lips, like French *tu* or German *ü*. Short *u* is a short, rounded sound, roughly like the vowel in "hurt" but quicker.

- De man kijkt naar de maan. — The man is looking at the moon.
- De boot is groot. — The boat is big.
- Mijn bus is te laat. — My bus is late.

## Open and closed syllables

A syllable that ends in a consonant is **closed** (*man*, *maan*). A syllable that ends in a vowel is **open** (*ma-* in *ma-nen*). The key rule:

**In an open syllable, a single vowel letter is already long.** So you only write *aa*, *ee*, *oo*, *uu* in closed syllables. That is why the spelling changes when a word gets an ending:

| short vowel | plural | long vowel | plural |
| ----------- | ------ | ---------- | ------ |
| man | man-nen | maan | ma-nen |
| bed | bed-den | been | be-nen |
| bot | bot-ten | boot | bo-ten |
| bus | bus-sen | muur | mu-ren |

To keep a short vowel short, you double the consonant after it: *mannen*, not *manen*. To keep a long vowel long, one letter is enough: *manen*, not *maanen*. The same rule works for verbs (*lopen → ik loop*) and adjectives (*groot → grote*).

At the end of a word, a single *a*, *o* or *u* is also long: *ja*, *zo*, *nu*. A single *e* at the end is a weak "uh" sound (*de*, *je*, *ze*); a long *ee* at the end is written double: *zee* (sea), *twee* (two).

## Vowel combinations

| spelling | sounds like | examples |
| -------- | ----------- | -------- |
| oe | "oo" in "food" | *boek* (book), *goed* (good) |
| ie | "ee" in "see" | *niet* (not), *vier* (four) |
| eu | "ay" with rounded lips (French *peu*) | *deur* (door), *neus* (nose) |
| ui | no English equivalent: "ow" with rounded lips and the tongue forward | *huis* (house), *uit* (out) |
| ij / ei | between "ay" in "day" and "eye" | *mijn* (my), *klein* (small) |
| ou / au | "ow" in "how" | *koud* (cold), *blauw* (blue) |

*ij* and *ei* sound exactly the same, and so do *ou* and *au*: you simply have to learn which spelling a word uses. Dutch people call them the *lange ij* and the *korte ei*. At the start of a word, *ij* is capitalised as one letter: *IJsland*, *het IJ*.

- Het huis is klein. — The house is small.
- Ik zit in de tuin. — I'm sitting in the garden.
- De deur is open. — The door is open.
- Het boek is goed. — The book is good.
- Mijn vrouw heeft een blauwe jas. — My wife has a blue coat.

## Consonants

**g and ch** are the same sound in most of the Netherlands: a rough sound at the back of the throat, like *ch* in Scottish "loch". In the south (Brabant, Limburg) and in Flanders, it is softer and further forward: the *zachte g*. The *g* is never like English "go", except in loanwords like *goal*.

**sch** is *s* + *ch*: *school* sounds like "s-chool", not "skool" or "shool". But at the end of a word, *-isch* is pronounced "-ies": *praktisch*, *logisch*.

**w** is not quite the English *w*. In the Netherlands, the lower lip lightly touches the upper teeth, as if you start a *v* but don't push. In Flanders and Suriname, it is closer to the English *w*.

**v** is pronounced almost like *f* by many speakers in the Netherlands, especially in the west: *vier* can sound like "fier". In Flanders, *v* and *f* are clearly different.

**b and d at the end** of a word sound like *p* and *t*: *hond* (dog) sounds like "hont". It is still written with *d* because the plural is *honden*.

- Goedemorgen! — Good morning!
- De school is dichtbij. — The school is close by.
- Wat wil je weten? — What do you want to know?
- Mijn vader werkt in de stad. — My father works in the city.

## The ending -en

In everyday speech in the Netherlands, the *n* of the ending *-en* is usually dropped: *lopen* sounds like "lope", and *we gaan eten* sounds like "we gaan ete". You still always write *-en*. In Flanders and in the north-east of the Netherlands, the *n* is pronounced more often.

- We gaan morgen fietsen. — We're going cycling tomorrow.

## Common mistakes

Reading *ij* as "ee" because it looks like *i*. Pronouncing *ui* as English "we" or "oo-ee": it is one sound that glides from "uh" towards a rounded vowel. Reading *eu* as "you", or *oe* as "oh". Saying the English "g". And in writing: *manen* (moons) when you mean *mannen* (men). If the vowel is short, double the consonant.
