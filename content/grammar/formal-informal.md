Dutch has two ways to say "you" to one person: informal *jij/je* and formal *u*. For more than one person, informal is *jullie*, and formal is again *u*.

| | informal | formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| subject | jij / je | u |
| object | jou / je | u |
| possessive | jouw / je | uw |
| plural | jullie | u |

## How u works

*u* takes the same verb form as *hij*: *u komt*, *u werkt*, *u bent*. With *hebben*, both *u hebt* and *u heeft* are correct; *u heeft* is more common. With *kunnen* and *willen*, *u kunt* and *u wilt* are the neat forms, but *u kan* and *u wil* are fine too.

Unlike *jij*, *u* keeps the *-t* when it comes after the verb: *Kom jij?* but *Komt u?*

- Kunt u mij helpen? — Can you help me?
- Waar woont u? — Where do you live?
- Heeft u even tijd? — Do you have a moment?
- Is dit uw jas? — Is this your coat?
- Komt u ook? — Are you coming too?

The same sentences with *je*:

- Kun je me helpen? — Can you help me?
- Waar woon je? — Where do you live?
- Heb je even tijd? — Do you have a moment?
- Is dit je jas? — Is this your coat?
- Hoe gaat het met jullie? — How are you all?

## Dutch people use je a lot

Many learners expect *u* to be the safe, polite default, as with French *vous* or German *Sie*. In the Netherlands, *je* is used much more widely: between colleagues (often including the boss), in shops, cafés and restaurants, between young people, at the gym, and in a lot of advertising and on many websites, including some government ones. Many adults feel that being called *u* makes them sound old.

*u* is still normal with older strangers, in formal letters and emails, with officials and doctors (though many younger doctors say *je*), and in customer service. Some families, especially older, religious or southern ones, have children say *u* to their grandparents or even to their parents. In prayers, God is addressed as *U*, with a capital letter.

A practical rule: start with *u* for older people you don't know and in formal situations, and switch as soon as the other person says *je* to you or invites you to.

- Zeg maar je, hoor. — You can just call me je.
- Mag ik je zeggen? — Can I call you je?
- Mogen we elkaar tutoyeren? — Shall we say je to each other?

The verbs for this are *tutoyeren* or *jijen en jouen* (saying *je*) and *vousvoyeren* (saying *u*).

## Don't mix them

Once you've chosen, stick to it for that person, also in possessives and reflexives. A very common learner mistake in emails is switching halfway.

- Kunt u uw naam en adres invullen? — Could you fill in your name and address?
- Wilt u zich even voorstellen? — Would you like to introduce yourself?

Not *Kunt u je naam invullen?* Also, *u* is used for a group in formal situations, for example in a shop or on a sign: *Wilt u hier wachten?* can be said to one person or to five.

## Flanders: gij and ge

In Belgium, *u* is used more often than in the Netherlands, also in some families. In everyday spoken Flemish, many people use *gij* (stressed) and *ge* (unstressed) as an informal "you", with *uw* as the possessive and verb forms like *gij zijt*, *gij hebt*. You'll hear it in films and on the street, but in standard Dutch, in writing and in the classroom, Flemish speakers use *jij/je* and *u* just like in the Netherlands.

- Gij hebt gelijk. — You're right. (spoken Flemish)
- Waar woont ge? — Where do you live? (spoken Flemish)

You don't need to use *gij* yourself, but it helps to recognise it.
